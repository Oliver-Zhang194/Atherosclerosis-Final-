Facts and Figures

  • 19.8 million deaths from cardiovascular disease (CVD) in 2022 (World Health Organization [WHO], 2025)
  • An estimated 57,100 acute coronary events in people 25 and over in 2023 (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [AIHW], 2026)
  • In 2024, coronary heart disease (CHD) was the underlying cause of 16,300 deaths (AIHW, 2026)

What Is Atherosclerosis?

Atherosclerosis is a chronic cardiovascular condition where a hardened plaque, made of fatty substances and particularly cholesterol, forms on the lining of an artery, partially or fully obstructing blood flow (Johns Hopkins Medicine, n.d.; Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute, 2025). Symptoms often develop later in life, most commonly after the age of 45. However, the condition progresses throughout your entire life, and symptoms can occur at any age (AIHW, 2026; Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute, 2025).

Myths and Truths

Myth: I'm too young to worry about my arteries.

Truth: Atherosclerosis develops slowly and can begin as early as childhood (Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute, 2025).

Myth: If I feel fine, my arteries are fine.

Truth: Atherosclerosis often causes no symptoms until a heart attack or stroke occurs (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; WHO, 2025).

Myth: A healthy lifestyle can clear plaque already in my arteries.

Truth: Lifestyle changes slow plaque growth but will not remove existing plaque (Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute, 2025).

How Do Plaques Form?

  1. Damage and Inflammation

    Plaques must form on the lining of damaged arteries (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Mayo Clinic, 2024). Damage can occur from a plethora of other cardiovascular conditions, most notably prolonged high blood pressure (hypertension) (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Mayo Clinic, 2024). The damaged artery triggers an immune response, where white blood cells (leukocytes) inflame the lining, roughening the surface (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Mayo Clinic, 2024).

  2. LDL Build-Up

    Circulating cholesterol in blood plasma (low-density lipoprotein or LDL) can snag onto the rough surface (Complete Anatomy, 2019). Specialised leukocytes called macrophages attempt to process the relatively large LDL molecules, in the process releasing cytokines (chemical signals) that further increase inflammation in the artery (Cleveland Clinic, 2024).

  3. Necrotic Core and Fibrous Cap

    The macrophages, once they have processed LDL, turn into foam cells that undergo programmed cell death (apoptosis), forming the soft centre of the plaque called the necrotic core (Cleveland Clinic, 2024). Given enough time, the smooth muscle lining the lumen (hollow part) of the artery is able to form a cover over the plaque, known as the fibrous cap (Cleveland Clinic, 2024).

  4. Rupture and Clotting

    If the plaque ruptures, the necrotic core will become exposed and the soft center can enter the blood stream and eventually clot. This can lead to acute, life-threatening symptoms such as a heart attack or stroke if the clot blocks a coronary artery or an artery supplying the brain (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute, 2025).

Risk Factors

The most influential risk factors include:

Hypertension, Diabetes, Smoking and Alcohol Consumption

These factors all influence inflammation and damage of the arteries, aiding in the formation of plaques (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Mayo Clinic, 2024; WHO, 2025).

36.1% of 18–24 year olds exceeded the alcohol guideline in 2022, the highest of any age group (Australian Bureau of Statistics [ABS], 2023a).

High Cholesterol, Obesity and Unhealthy Diet (high saturated fat, sugar and sodium)

These factors influence the amount of LDL circulating, and therefore the ability of plaques to form (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Mayo Clinic, 2024; WHO, 2025).

42.1% of 18–24 year olds were overweight or obese in 2022 (ABS, 2023b).

Family History of Cardiovascular Disease, Age (men over 45 and women over 55) and Male Sex

(AIHW, 2026; Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Mayo Clinic, 2024)

Prevention

Preventative measures cannot clear existing plaques from arteries. However, they can slow down the rate at which they progress and prevent new plaques from forming (Johns Hopkins Medicine, n.d.; Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute, 2025). After adjusting for age, the rate of acute coronary events fell by 61% between 2001 and 2023, which the AIHW links to falls in smoking, high cholesterol and high blood pressure (AIHW, 2026). The most effective prevention measures include:

Changing to a Healthy Diet (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute, 2025)

Eat foods low in saturated fats, lowering circulating LDL.

Quitting Smoking/Vaping and Reducing Excessive Alcohol Consumption (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; WHO, 2025)

Smoking damages the lining of the artery, where plaques start, and harmful alcohol use is a major risk factor for heart disease (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; WHO, 2025).

Symptoms

Long-term symptoms build up as an artery slowly narrows, while short-term symptoms appear suddenly, usually when a plaque ruptures and a clot blocks the artery (Mayo Clinic, 2024; Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute, 2025).

Long-Term Symptoms

Temporary and/or Long-Lasting Chest Pain (Angina)

A partial blockage in a coronary artery reduces the oxygen reaching the heart, which causes pain (AIHW, 2026; Cleveland Clinic, 2024).

Pain or Numbness in the Extremities

A blockage in a large artery can reduce blood flow to a limb, causing pain or numbness (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Mayo Clinic, 2024).

Short-Term Symptoms

Dizziness, Light-Headedness, Nausea, Vomiting and Loss of Consciousness

Reduced blood flow to the brain will lower brain function, causing the above symptoms (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; WHO, 2025).

Sweating

Reduced blood flow to the heart will trigger the sympathetic nervous system (the fight-or-flight reflex), causing sweating (WHO, 2025).

Heart Attack and Stroke

A ruptured plaque could send a clot to a coronary artery or an artery of the brain. If it fully blocks (occludes) the vessel, the tissue will die, causing a heart attack or stroke (AIHW, 2026; Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Mayo Clinic, 2024).

If you or someone else is experiencing loss of consciousness, a heart attack or a stroke, call 000 immediately

Treatments

Treatments fall under the categories of medicinal and procedural (Cleveland Clinic, 2024). Four main medicines are usually prescribed for atherosclerosis. Antiplatelet and anticoagulant medicines reduce clotting around a plaque, so a rupture is less likely to block the artery. Cholesterol-lowering medicines reduce circulating LDL, so less builds up in the artery wall. Blood pressure medicines lower the force on the artery lining, so there is less damage (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Johns Hopkins Medicine, n.d.).

Procedures are minimally to moderately invasive, and most commonly a form of angioplasty is used (Cleveland Clinic, 2024; Johns Hopkins Medicine, n.d.). Types of angioplasties include:

Balloon

A small balloon is inflated in the artery, opening up the lumen (Johns Hopkins Medicine, n.d.).

Atherectomy

A small part of the plaque is shaved away (Johns Hopkins Medicine, n.d.).

Laser

A laser is used to vaporise the plaque (Johns Hopkins Medicine, n.d.).

Coronary Artery Stent

A metal mesh tube is used to hold open the lumen (Johns Hopkins Medicine, n.d.).

Effectiveness, Pros and Cons

Cholesterol-lowering medicines can shrink plaques and make unstable plaques stable, but it takes aggressive treatment and removing plaque completely is very difficult (Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute, 2025).

Angioplasty and stents can restore blood flow, but they are mainly used when a blockage is severe or the risk of complications is high (Cleveland Clinic, 2024).

A healthy lifestyle remains the best treatment, with medicines and procedures used when necessary (Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute, 2025).
